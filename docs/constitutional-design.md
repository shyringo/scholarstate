# Constitutional Design For AI-Native Research

ScholarState is not complete if it only stores better metadata. A research system shapes what gets produced, checked, funded, remembered, and corrected. Its protocol should therefore serve a constitutional design rather than quietly hard-code one platform's incentives.

## Purpose Of Scientific Infrastructure

Scientific infrastructure should help communities reduce consequential uncertainty through processes that remain testable, traceable, reusable, and correctable. Prediction, explanation, understanding, and action are different outcomes; no single metric captures all of them. Research also operates under human values and finite resources, so uncertainty reduction alone cannot determine which questions deserve attention.

## The Scarcity Shift

AI lowers the cost of drafting text, code, hypotheses, analyses, and plausible criticism. It does not lower every cost equally. The new bottlenecks include:

- choosing questions whose answers matter;
- obtaining trusted observations and access to the physical or social world;
- distinguishing genuine evidence from persuasive volume;
- reproducing results across relevant conditions;
- resolving disagreements without erasing minority interpretations;
- accepting responsibility for decisions that allocate status or resources;
- maintaining public knowledge after the original authors leave.

A platform that rewards the count or velocity of outputs amplifies the wrong side of this imbalance.

## Three Planes

```text
record plane          interpretation plane       institution plane
-------------         --------------------       -----------------
immutable events ---> profiles and projections -> scoped decisions
artifacts              evidence rules             due process
provenance             living syntheses           resource allocation
versions               competing views            appeals and recognition
```

The record plane should be maximally portable and conservative. The interpretation plane should be plural, versioned, and forkable. The institution plane should be explicitly human-accountable where decisions gain academic or material force.

No plane should silently absorb the others. In particular, a convenient projection must not rewrite the record, and a high-confidence model output must not become institutional authority by default.

## Constitutional Constraints

1. **No invisible overwrites.** Correction adds history; it does not erase history.
2. **No universal scalar credibility.** Trust is conditional on claim, evidence, method, scope, community, and time.
3. **No authority from volume.** Generating more objects, citations, agents, or votes does not create standing.
4. **No forced ontology.** Communities can share records while disagreeing about their interpretation.
5. **No orphaned formal power.** Every consequential decision terminates in identifiable human responsibility and a contestable policy.
6. **No extraction without the source.** Structured representations remain linked to losslessly preserved material whenever preservation is lawful and possible.
7. **No popularity proxy for truth.** Social reach can aid discovery but cannot serve as evidence.
8. **No irreversible institutional lock-in.** Records, profiles, and recognized decisions must be exportable; communities retain meaningful rights to fork and exit.
9. **No costless verification burden.** Actors who introduce large volumes of claims should expose scope, uncertainty, and evidence so validation resources can be allocated deliberately.
10. **No permanent status without maintenance.** A status is a dated projection under identified rules, not an eternal label.
11. **No compulsory atomization.** Fine structure is created when independent scientific use justifies its identity and verification cost, not merely because a machine can extract it.
12. **No compulsory global replication.** The right to publish does not create an obligation for every community to store, index, review, or recognize an object.

## Incentive Direction

Credit should attach to useful acts across the research lifecycle: forming important questions, producing evidence, building methods and tools, reproducing results, finding boundary conditions, publishing failures, correcting records, synthesizing fields, and maintaining infrastructure. CRediT or other vocabularies can describe roles, but each community must decide what it values.

Resource allocation should optimize expected value of information under real constraints, not engagement. Candidate signals include decision relevance, uncertainty, downstream dependency, verification cost, neglectedness, and potential harm. These signals must remain inspectable and plural; collapsing them into one ranking would invite gaming and conceal value judgments.

One promising mechanism is a **verification budget**: high-throughput claim production is paired with explicit compute, experiment, reviewer, or funding commitments for checking the resulting burden. This is a research hypothesis, not yet a protocol requirement. Field trials should determine whether it improves correction rates or merely privileges wealthy actors.

The counterweight is **progressive crystallization**: legacy material enters losslessly at its existing granularity; cheap candidate structure is computed on demand; only fragments that acquire independent use become durable objects. See [granularity](granularity.md).

## AI And Institutional Sovereignty

AI may search, extract, translate, formalize, propose experiments, execute permitted workflows, compare evidence, and draft decisions. Every output keeps machine provenance. A human may accept responsibility for a machine-assisted act, but the record must preserve both the machine's role and the human acceptance rather than relabeling the work as purely human.

Institutional authority comes from a community's legitimate process, not from intelligence, accuracy, model ownership, or computational scale. Human control is not a claim that humans are always more correct. It is the assignment of contestable responsibility in institutions built by and for people.

## Evaluation

The project should be judged by observed outcomes, not schema coverage:

- Can users find why a claim is believed and where its boundary lies?
- Do corrections propagate to downstream views without destroying history?
- Are negative and maintenance contributions visible and rewarded?
- Can communities disagree without fragmenting the underlying evidence record?
- Does formal power remain attributable, appealable, and scoped?
- Does AI reduce synthesis cost without causing verification debt to grow faster?

If ScholarState cannot improve these outcomes in a real field trial, the correct response is to change or abandon its mechanisms, not defend the elegance of its data model.
